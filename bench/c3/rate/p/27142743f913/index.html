The Ferry That Ran on Faith and Fence Wire

For sixty-one years, a flat-bottomed barge and one stubborn family carried Millbrook across Alder Creek. The bridge that replaced it took four months to build — and the town is still deciding what it lost.

Written by
Marguerite Oyelaran

On the morning of April 3, 1959, Ruth Haldane winched the ferry across Alder Creek for the last time. There were eleven passengers, two milk cans, and a borrowed Ford pickup on the deck. Nobody made a speech. Her nephew remembers that she checked the cable twice, the way she always did, and that she did not look at the new steel bridge rising a quarter mile downstream.

The crossing had been in her family since 1898, when her grandfather, Elias Haldane, strung a salvaged telegraph cable between two cottonwoods and built a barge from barn timbers. The county never owned it. For most of its life, the Alder Creek ferry ran on a handshake agreement, a ledger of unpaid fares, and a coil of fence wire that patched everything from the guide rail to the toll box hinge.

A ledger of the unpaid

The family’s fare book survives in Box 41 of the county archive, its spine split and resewn with — of course — wire. Between 1931 and 1937, nearly a third of the entries read simply “on account.” Some debts were settled in eggs, some in labour, and one, from a schoolteacher named Clara Voss, in a winter of reading lessons for the Haldane children.

“Grandmother said the ferry wasn’t a business. It was a promise that the other side of the creek would always be there.”

— Thomas Haldane, interviewed October 2026

When the state proposed the bridge in 1956, the town meeting ran past midnight. Farmers on the east bank wanted the road; shopkeepers on the west bank feared it would carry customers straight past them toward the county seat. Both turned out to be right.
