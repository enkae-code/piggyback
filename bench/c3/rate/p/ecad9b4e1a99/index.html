Streets & waterways

The river under Market Street

The mill race was bricked over in 1838. Nobody told the water.

Words by Maren Holt Drawing by Tobias Wren 22 November 2026 Nine minutes to read

For most of the nineteenth century, the people of Lowfield walked to market over a river and never knew it. The mill race that once turned the Corn Exchange wheel was cut in 1702, ran open along what is now Market Street for a hundred and thirty years, and was then roofed in brick by a town council tired of the smell and the drownings. The minutes record the cost to the penny and the length to the foot. They do not record anyone asking where the water would go instead.

It went exactly where it had always gone. The arch the council paid for1 is nine feet across and still dry above the waterline, laid by a Lowfield firm whose bricks carry a thumbprint-sized stamp. Three drains feed it. One manhole2 outside number 14 opens onto it, and on a wet night you can hear it through the iron.

The clues on the surface are small. The gas lamp3 on the west pavement stands a foot further from the kerb than its neighbours, because it was set against the old parapet wall. The east kerb has a millstone4 in it, quietly reused. Once you have seen them, the whole street reads as a bank.

Ruth Askew, who ran the ironmonger’s at the corner from 1951 until 1989, remembers the cellar flooding every March. “My father called it the spring tide,” she says. “He kept the stock on bricks. We all did. Nobody thought of it as a river, it was just the cellar being the cellar.”
