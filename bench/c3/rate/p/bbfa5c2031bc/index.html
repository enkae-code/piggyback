The hall that weighed a valley

How the Harlow Street Corn Exchange outlived its trade.

Words by Maud Ellery Drawing by Tomas Rein 14 November 2026

On market Thursdays in 1871 the farmers of the Ash valley queued from the bridge to the door of the new Corn Exchange, each with a sample bag of grain tucked under one arm. The hall was built for a single purpose: to put every bushel in the valley under the same north light and the same set of scales, so that no merchant could claim a dark corner made the wheat look better than it was.

The trade it served lasted barely sixty years. Rail freight, then the county co-operative, emptied the floor by 1934. Yet the building did not fall with it. The granary wall at its east end,1 laid in local brick a full yard thick, has carried a seed store, a drill hall, a skating rink and a jumble of wartime ration offices.

Much of the original kit simply stayed put. The lantern2 still throws its even light along the ridge, and the brass weighing beam3 hangs from the first truss, though nobody living remembers it set to a true pound.

Walk the length of the hall today and the floor keeps the best record. The York stone flags4 are worn into shallow troughs where the stalls once stood, and the dips line up, row by row, with an 1880 rent book held at the county archive.

  1. Granary wall: brick, a yard thick, 1868.
  2. Roof lantern, glazed on all four sides.
  3. The brass weighing beam, still hung.
  4. York stone flags, worn by the stalls.
