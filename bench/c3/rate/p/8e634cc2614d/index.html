Lead story — Restoration

The Quiet Load-Bearing Wall

In a river town that forgot how to build, a cooperative of masons spent four winters rebuilding a granary with lime, timber and patience — and relearned a way of making that no drawing could hold.

Words by Ines Aldecoa Drawings by Tomas Reyl 18 November 2026 14 min read

Building
Vellmar Granary
Built
c. 1780; rebuilt 2022–26
Makers
Low Water Masons’ Cooperative
Materials
Local limestone, hot lime, green oak

The first thing the masons did was not build. For most of the opening winter they took the old granary apart stone by stone, numbering each in chalk, laying them out across the flood meadow like the pieces of a sentence that had been dropped and needed reading again. Nobody in the town had laid a wall this way for sixty years. The drawings they inherited showed where the walls went, but not how they stood.

What stood them up, it turned out, was the mortar. The granary had been built in hot lime — quicklime slaked in the mix, still steaming as it went onto the stone — and the walls had spent two centuries breathing, flexing with the river damp, healing their own hairline cracks. The cement repairs of the 1970s had stopped all that. Water that once moved through the wall got trapped behind the patches, and the stone began to fail from the inside.

“You can’t draw a wall that breathes. You can only learn to listen to one.”

— Marta Ilić, lead mason

Four winters, one material

The cooperative works only between November and March, when the river is high and farm work is light. That rhythm shaped everything: lime needs slow, damp curing, and the cold months gave it time. Each spring the scaffolding came down and the town walked around what had been made, and each autumn the masons came back to find what the summer had taught the wall.

There is a temptation, writing about a project like this, to make it a parable about the old ways. The masons resist that. They used laser levels and moisture meters. They argued about spreadsheets. What they refused was the idea that a building is finished when the drawing is.
