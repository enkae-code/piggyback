Rooms with a longer life

On buildings that improve with use.

  • Words by Rowan Bell
  • Drawing by Ines Achterberg
  • Eight minutes to read

Lena Ortiz did not set out to design a landmark. The repair shop on Calder Street was fourteen feet wide, lit from one side, and likely to change hands every few years for as long as it stood. The brief she wrote for herself fits on an index card: leave the next tenant fewer walls to knock down.

So the plan keeps its services at the yard end. Water, power and the stair sit in a single brick spine,1 and everything else is furniture. A bindery worked here first, then a bicycle co-op, then for one winter a night school for welders. None of them had to call a builder.

What reads as restraint is closer to bookkeeping. Ortiz priced each fixed element against the years it would outlast its purpose, and removed whatever lost. The roof lights2 open by hand, with a pole3 that hangs beside the door. The floor is end-grain oak4 because oak can be sanded forty times.

Rooms like this rarely photograph well. They are better judged in their tenth year, when the marks on the floor show where people chose to stand.

Key to the section

  1. Service spine: water, power and the stair, in brick.
  2. Roof lights, facing north.
  3. The pole that opens them, on its peg.
